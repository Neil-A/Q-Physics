# The Idea, In Plain Terms

*Rewritten 9 August 2026 to match [FRAMEWORK.md](FRAMEWORK.md) after the 6 August simulations ([RESULTS-01.md](RESULTS-01.md)). The previous version described a gravity-and-time story cut in July and a handshake mechanism that has since failed its test. Both are gone from here.*

## The picture

Past and future both exist. But the future exists as *possibility*, not as settled fact.

The "present" isn't a razor-thin instant. It's a zone with **thickness** — and thickness means *how much is still undecided* there. A rock is thin: almost everything about it is already settled. An electron in flight is thick: where it will land is still open.

The universe moves forward by **settling** what's undecided. That settling zone — the **front** — sweeps forward through the block.

**Scope:** this is a framework about the double slit and the measurement problem. Nothing else. Gravity-as-origin and cosmology were cut, and should not come back.

## What a particle is

An unresolved region — a **blob**. Inside it, possibilities coexist and oscillate. The blob can stretch: part of it reaching ahead into the future, part trailing behind in the past. Being one connected thing is why entangled particles don't need to send each other signals.

Mathematically the blob is just the wavefunction. That's worth saying plainly — it isn't a new object.

## What thickness actually is

Thickness is **coherence**: a standard, well-defined quantity from quantum information theory that measures how much superposition is present.

This was tested rather than asserted. It behaves correctly: maximum for a live superposition, exactly zero for a dot on a screen, falling smoothly in between. And it runs *opposite* to how much the environment has learned — which is the right way round, because learning is settling.

Two honest limits, both found by running the numbers:

- It works as a measure of "this blob got thinner." It does **not** work as "blob A is thicker than blob B" for two unrelated blobs. Different valid measures disagree about that.
- It's defined for a chosen set of alternatives — here, which slit. That sounds like it might be a cheat, and it isn't: for the cases that matter, the which-slit description turns out to be the *most generous* one available. The blob is thin no matter how you look at it.

## What "measurement" is

A blob settles when something in the world ends up *carrying a record of which possibility happened*. Not consciousness — just correlation.

- Nothing records which slit the electron took → it stays unsettled → interference pattern.
- A detector records the slit → settled immediately → pattern disappears.

Does a chair come out thin? Yes, overwhelmingly. Coherence collapses exponentially as more of the environment couples in. For an object with a chair's number of particles the number is zero for every practical purpose and then some.

## The one real bet: gravity never settles anything

Gravity touches everything, but it can't tell the two paths apart — both slit-paths carry identical mass. So gravity is a coupling that records nothing, and by the rule above it settles nothing. **Gravity does not collapse wavefunctions, at any mass scale.**

The simulation checked this internally and it held exactly, not approximately: with a coupling that can't distinguish the paths, coherence stays at 1.000000000000 — no settling whatsoever, at any environment size, at any time. Settling *is* precisely the distinguishability of the record.

This matters because it contradicts two live things:

- **The Diósi–Penrose family**, which says gravity causes collapse. Currently under experimental test.
- **Strubbe (2025)**, whose model predicts you could pull which-slit information out gravitationally *without* wrecking the interference pattern. Same experiment, opposite prediction, named opponent.

That disagreement is checkable, and it's now the framework's only claim that could be proved wrong by an experiment.

## What was tried and didn't work

The framework used to predict that a blob's tail closes when a "confirmation" returns from wherever the particle is heading — so coherence should last longer when the destination is further away, on a timescale of distance ÷ speed of light.

**This was simulated on 6 August and it's false.** Coherence lifetime doesn't track distance at all. It's set by the local coupling strength and nothing else. In one configuration the effect ran the *wrong way* — a nearer absorber preserved more coherence.

Worse, it can't be rescued by tuning: coherence decays at the local rate, and news from a distant absorber can't arrive faster than light, so past a certain distance the absorber's location *cannot* matter in any theory at all.

Something real does scale with distance — how long it takes the record to form at the far end. But that's just travel time. Every theory has it. It distinguishes nothing.

Recording this properly is worth something. It was a clean prediction, it was cheap to test, and closing it took an afternoon instead of a rejection letter.

## What's still unfinished

**The selection problem**, and it's the big one. Outcomes are taken to be **determined**, not random — fixed by conditions inside the blob. That's a legitimate position; it's the family Bohmian mechanics belongs to, and Bell only forbids *local* determinism. But it owes two things:

1. Whatever picks the winner must pick at exactly the rate observed — amplitude squared. Not roughly. Exactly. Explaining *why*, rather than assuming it, would be the real contribution.
2. Those conditions must be permanently unmeasurable, or you could steer outcomes and signal faster than light.

Nobody has solved this. Cramer didn't in 1986, and Strubbe doesn't either — he decides outcomes with a random number he declares uniform, which produces the right statistics because he chose the distribution that produces them. It is worth being clear that this framework hasn't solved it either.

## Honest status

The metaphysics hangs together, and the machinery has now survived contact with a computer: thickness is a real, well-behaved quantity; the settling rule works exactly; the standard bounds it relies on all reproduce.

But the blob is the wavefunction under a new name, "the front converts coherence into entanglement" is a theorem someone else proved, and the one prediction that was distinctively ours failed its test.

**What's left is one sharp bet against a named opponent on one experiment, plus a coherent interpretation of standard open-system quantum mechanics.** That's a smaller claim than the project set out to make. It's also one that's been tested rather than argued.
